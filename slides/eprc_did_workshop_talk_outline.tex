\slideheading{3}{Traditional DID Setup}
\mainpoint{1(a): one example of traditional DID.}

\slidecontent{In the simplest DID design, we compare two groups across two
periods. One group receives treatment after the pre-period. The other group is
not treated. DID asks whether the treated group changes more after treatment
than the comparison group changes over the same period. In the synthetic data,
the treated group is districts that implement the G\&T-style program in year 5.}

\[
\widehat{\delta}_{DID}
=
(\bar{Y}_{T,post} - \bar{Y}_{T,pre})
-
(\bar{Y}_{C,post} - \bar{Y}_{C,pre}).
\]

\slidecontent{\textbf{Concept.} DID is not just treated versus comparison after
treatment. It is the treated group's change minus the comparison group's
change.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.18\textwidth}p{0.18\textwidth}p{0.24\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Group} & \textbf{Pre} & \textbf{Post} & \textbf{Change} \\
\midrule
Treated & Mean before year 5 & Mean after year 5 & Treated change. \\
Comparison & Mean before year 5 & Mean after year 5 & Comparison change. \\
DID & & & Treated change minus comparison change. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 3: two-group, two-period DID structure.}
\end{table}

\slideheading{4}{Parallel Trends: The Identifying Assumption}
\mainpoint{1(a): use a graph to show the idea of parallel trends.}

\slidecontent{\textbf{Key identifying idea.} DID asks what would have happened
to treated units after treatment if they had not been treated. The comparison
group is useful if it tells us what would have happened to the treated group in
the absence of treatment. Parallel trends is therefore an assumption about
untreated potential outcomes, not about the observed post-treatment outcomes.}

\[
E[Y_{T,post}(0) - Y_{T,pre}(0)]
=
E[Y_{C,post}(0) - Y_{C,pre}(0)].
\]

\slidecontent{In words: without the G\&T-style program, treated districts would
have experienced the same average change in achievement as comparison districts.}

\plannedfig{simple_parallel_trends.png}{0.74}{Planned figure for Slide 4: raw group trends from the synthetic data. Treated units adopt in year 5; comparison units are never treated.}

\slideheading{5}{Classic DID Picture: Observed vs. Counterfactual}
\mainpoint{1(a): use a traditional DID graph to show observed treated outcomes, the counterfactual trend, and the DID effect.}

\slidecontent{This is the classic DID teaching figure. The treated group has an
observed post-treatment outcome, but the missing object is the treated group's
counterfactual post-treatment outcome: where would the treated group have been
without treatment?}

\slidecontent{\textbf{Potential outcomes.} For district $i$ in year $t$,
$Y_{it}(1)$ is the potential outcome with the program and $Y_{it}(0)$ is the
potential outcome without the program. For treated districts after year 5, we
observe $Y_{it}(1)$, but we do not observe $Y_{it}(0)$. DID uses the comparison
group trend to approximate that missing counterfactual.}

\[
\widehat{Y}_{T,post}(0)
=
\bar{Y}_{T,pre}
+
(\bar{Y}_{C,post} - \bar{Y}_{C,pre}).
\]

\[
\widehat{\delta}_{DID}
=
\bar{Y}_{T,post}
-
\widehat{Y}_{T,post}(0).
\]

\[
ATT =
E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid G_i=1].
\]

\slidecontent{Standard DID usually targets the average treatment effect on the
treated (ATT): the average effect for districts that actually implemented the
program.}

\plannedfig{classic_did_counterfactual.png}{0.74}{Planned figure for Slide 5: comparison trend, observed treated trend, dashed treated counterfactual trend, and the DID estimate.}

\slideheading{6}{DID as a Regression}
\mainpoint{1(a), bridge: connect the traditional DID example to the regression version before introducing TWFE.}

\slidecontent{The same two-by-two contrast can be estimated with a regression.
The coefficient on the interaction between treatment group and post period is
the DID estimate. Because the data are generated, we can compare the estimated
effect with the known simulated effect.}

\[
Y_{it}
=
\alpha
+
\beta Treat_i
+
\gamma Post_t
+
\delta(Treat_i \times Post_t)
+
\varepsilon_{it}.
\]

\section*{Part B. TWFE and Event Studies}

\slideheading{7}{Introduce the TWFE Model}
\mainpoint{1(b)(i): introduce the TWFE model.}

\slidecontent{Many applied DID papers estimate a two-way fixed effects (TWFE)
model. Instead of only using one pre-period and one post-period, TWFE uses the
full panel and controls for unit fixed effects and time fixed effects.}

\[
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \delta D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}.
\]

\slidecontent{\textbf{Concept.} TWFE is the fixed-effects version of the DID
regression. In the simple one-treatment-cohort case, the treatment indicator is
the same treated-after-treatment idea as $Treat_i \times Post_t$. The model is
attractive because it uses repeated observations and absorbs stable district
differences and common year shocks.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.27\textwidth}p{0.55\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model piece} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
$\alpha_i$ & Unit fixed effects: stable differences across districts. \\
$\lambda_t$ & Year fixed effects: shocks common to all districts in a year. \\
$D_{it}$ & Treatment indicator that equals 1 for treated districts after treatment begins. \\
$\delta$ & Estimated treatment effect in the simple setup. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 7: pieces of the TWFE model.}
\end{table}

\[
\widehat{\delta}
=
(\overline{Y}_{T,post}-\overline{Y}_{T,pre})
-
(\overline{Y}_{C,post}-\overline{Y}_{C,pre}).
\]

\slidecontent{In a balanced panel with one common treatment start and
never-treated comparison districts, the TWFE coefficient recovers the familiar
DID contrast. The pre and post means can average over multiple years, not just
one pre year and one post year.}

\slideheading{8}{Event Studies: Re-centering Time Around Treatment}
\mainpoint{1(b)(ii): introduce event-study models.}

\slidecontent{An event study replaces the single treatment indicator with
indicators for time relative to treatment. This lets us look at outcomes before
and after treatment. In the synthetic data, event time 0 is year 5, when treated
districts implement the G\&T-style program.}

\[
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t
+
\sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \mathbf{1}[t - G_i = k]
+
\varepsilon_{it}.
\]

\slidecontent{Here, $G_i$ is the year district $i$ first receives treatment and
$k=t-G_i$ is event time. The indicator $\mathbf{1}[t-G_i=k]$ equals 1 when the
district-year observation is exactly $k$ years from treatment, and 0 otherwise.
Never-treated districts have all event-time indicators equal to 0.}

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
\text{Calendar year } t & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 \\
\hline
\text{Treatment year } G_i & 5 & 5 & 5 & 5 & 5 & 5 & 5 \\
\text{Event time } k=t-G_i & -4 & -3 & -2 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 2
\end{array}
\]

\slidecontent{Event time $k=-1$ is omitted because it is the reference period:
the year immediately before treatment. For example, for a district treated in
year 5, $\mathbf{1}[t-G_i=2]=1$ in year 7 because $7-5=2$.}

\slidecontent{\textbf{Concept.} Pre-treatment coefficients show whether treated
and comparison districts were already diverging before treatment. Post-treatment
coefficients show whether effects appear immediately, grow, or fade after
treatment.}

\plannedfig{normalized_event_time_trends.png}{0.62}{Planned figure for Slide 8: outcomes centered at event time -1. The plot should label $k=-1$ as the reference year, $k=0$ as treatment begins, the left side as pre-treatment, and the right side as post-treatment.}

\slideheading{9}{Limitation of a Single TWFE Coefficient}
\mainpoint{1(b)(iii): explain the limitation of TWFE models - one constant ATT can hide dynamic or heterogeneous effects.}

\slidecontent{A single TWFE coefficient is simple, but it compresses many
possible effects into one number. This can be misleading if effects grow, fade,
or appear only several years after treatment.}

\slidecontent{In the simple design, TWFE is close to the familiar DID contrast.
Once treatment timing varies, TWFE can compare newly treated units to
never-treated units, not-yet-treated units, and already-treated units in ways
that are harder to interpret.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.53\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{What TWFE gives us} & \textbf{What it can hide} \\
\midrule
One treatment coefficient & Effects that change over time. \\
A simple average & Differences across treatment cohorts. \\
A familiar regression model & Comparisons involving already-treated units. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 9: why one TWFE coefficient can be too compressed.}
\end{table}

\section*{Part C. Staggered DID Preview}

\slideheading{10}{Staggered DID Example: G\&T Entry at Different Times}
\mainpoint{1(c): one example of staggered DID, G\&T, with one graph to show different treatment timing.}

\slidecontent{In many education settings, treatment does not start for everyone
at the same time. Students may enter G\&T at different grade points. Districts
or schools may adopt a policy in different years.}

\slidecontent{In the synthetic data, one treated group adopts in year 5 and
another adopts in year 8. A third group is never treated. This mirrors the core
staggered-adoption problem: treatment timing is itself part of the design.}

\plannedfig{staggered_timing_plot.png}{0.72}{Planned figure for Slide 10: early adopters, later adopters, and never-treated units.}

\clearpage

\slideheading{11}{Why Staggered Timing Complicates TWFE}
\mainpoint{1(c)(i): discussion of the problem of TWFE - Bacon decomposition.}

\slidecontent{With staggered timing, TWFE no longer compares one treated group
to one untreated group. Instead, the regression uses variation in treatment
status across groups and years. That means the comparison group changes
depending on which cohort and time period we are looking at.}

\slidecontent{In the synthetic data, there are three treatment-timing groups:}

\[
G_i=5 \quad \text{early adopters}
\]

\[
G_i=8 \quad \text{later adopters}
\]

\[
G_i=\infty \quad \text{never-treated districts}.
\]

\plannedfig{staggered_group_trends.png}{0.70}{Planned figure for Slide 11: early-treated, later-treated, and never-treated group trends. The figure helps students see when each group is untreated, newly treated, or already treated.}

\slidecontent{\textbf{Goodman-Bacon decomposition.} With staggered adoption,
the TWFE coefficient can be written as a weighted average of many smaller
two-by-two DID comparisons.}

\[
\widehat{\beta}_{TWFE}
=
\sum_j w_j \widehat{\beta}^{2\times2}_j.
\]

\slidecontent{The key question is: what comparisons are inside that weighted
average? Some comparisons are intuitive; others are problematic.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.18\textwidth}p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.46\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{Years used} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
$G=5$ vs. never & Years 5--11 &
Early adopters compared with districts that are never treated. This is the most
intuitive DID comparison. \\
$G=8$ vs. never & Years 8--11 &
Later adopters compared with districts that are never treated. This is also an
intuitive DID comparison. \\
$G=5$ vs. $G=8$ & Years 5--7 &
Early adopters compared with later adopters before the later adopters are
treated. The later group is not-yet-treated, so this can be a useful comparison. \\
\textcolor{red}{$G=8$ vs. $G=5$} & \textcolor{red}{Years 8--11} &
\textcolor{red}{Later adopters compared with early adopters after the early
adopters are already treated. This is the problematic comparison.} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 11: examples of the comparisons inside a TWFE estimate with staggered treatment timing.}
\end{table}

\slidecontent{The problematic comparison is sometimes called a
\textbf{forbidden comparison}: TWFE may compare newly treated districts to
already-treated districts. But already-treated districts are not untreated
controls. If treatment effects evolve over time, those districts may already
have accumulated treatment effects.}

\slidecontent{This is why the single TWFE coefficient can become hard to
interpret. It is not simply ``the average effect of the G\&T-style program.''
It is a weighted average of several DID comparisons, and the weights can be
unequal, hard to interpret, or even negative.}

\slidecontent{\textbf{Takeaway.} In staggered DID, the problem is not only that
there are multiple treatment timings. The problem is that TWFE chooses the
comparisons and weights for us. Modern staggered DID estimators try to estimate
cohort-time effects directly and then aggregate them using clearer choices.}

\clearpage

\slideheading{12}{Staggered DID Estimator: CSA Logic}
\mainpoint{1(c)(ii)(A): CSA - group units into 2-by-2 designs, estimate ATT(g,t), then take a weighted average.}

\slidecontent{Modern staggered DID estimators avoid relying on one opaque TWFE
coefficient. The Callaway and Sant'Anna logic is to define treatment cohorts,
estimate group-time treatment effects, and then aggregate those effects in a way
that matches the research question.}

\[
ATT(g,t)
=
E[Y_t(g) - Y_t(0) \mid G_i = g].
\]

\slidecontent{For group $g$, first treated in period $g$, estimate the effect at
time $t$ by comparing that group to appropriate untreated or not-yet-treated
units.}

\[
ATT
=
\sum_{g,t} w_{gt} ATT(g,t).
\]

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.28\textwidth}p{0.54\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Object} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
$G_i = g$ & Unit $i$ first receives treatment in period $g$. \\
$ATT(g,t)$ & Effect for cohort $g$ at calendar time $t$. \\
$w_{gt}$ & Weight used to summarize group-time effects. \\
$ATT$ & Weighted average of selected group-time treatment effects. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 12: CSA-style group-time estimand and weighted aggregation.}
\end{table}

\slideheading{13}{CSA Aggregations: Group, Simple, and Event-Time ATT}
\mainpoint{1(c)(ii)(A)(i): group, simple, and aggregate ATT summaries.}

\slidecontent{After estimating $ATT(g,t)$, the next question is what summary to
report. The \texttt{did} package often describes these as \texttt{aggte()}
summaries. The important idea for the workshop is that the same group-time
effects can be aggregated in different ways.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.27\textwidth}p{0.55\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Aggregation} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
Simple ATT & Weighted average of all relevant post-treatment $ATT(g,t)$ values. \\
Group ATT & Average effect by treatment cohort or adoption group. \\
Dynamic / event-time ATT & Average effect by time since treatment. \\
Calendar ATT & Average effect in a given calendar period. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\end{table}
