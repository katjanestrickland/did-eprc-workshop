\section*{Overview}

This document drafts the main content for each slide in the DID EPRC workshop
talk. It is not the final slide deck. Each entry gives the slide-facing text and
then shows the table, equation, or figure that would appear on that slide.

The empirical motivation is Strickland, Chan, and Gottfried (2026), an
\emph{Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis} paper on NYC's Gifted and
Talented (G\&T) program using a staggered difference-in-differences design. The
workshop exercises use synthetic panel data generated for teaching, inspired by
that setting; they are not the NYC administrative data used in the paper.

\slideheading{3}{What Data Do We Need for DID?}

\slidecontent{To estimate DID, we need repeated observations on units over time,
an outcome, treatment status, and a way to define pre- and post-treatment
periods. In the synthetic example, the unit is a district and the policy turns
on for treated districts in a known year.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.23\textwidth}p{0.32\textwidth}p{0.27\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{DID ingredient} & \textbf{G\&T interpretation} & \textbf{Synthetic data variable} \\
\midrule
Unit & Student, school, or district & \texttt{district\_id} \\
Time & Year or grade & \texttt{year} \\
Outcome & Achievement or attendance & \texttt{outcome} \\
Treatment group & Exposed to G\&T or policy & \texttt{treated\_ever} \\
Post period & After entry or adoption & \texttt{post} \\
Treatment timing & First exposure year & \texttt{treatment\_start\_year} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 3: variables needed for the synthetic DID example.}
\end{table}

\slideheading{4}{Traditional DID and Parallel Trends}

\slidecontent{\textbf{Key identifying idea.} DID asks what would have happened
to treated units after treatment if they had not been treated. The comparison
group is useful if, before treatment, it was moving in parallel with the treated
group. In this simple synthetic example, one group adopts treatment in year 5
and one group is never treated.}

\plannedfig{simple_parallel_trends.png}{0.76}{Planned figure for Slide 4: treated units adopt in year 5; comparison units are never treated.}

\slideheading{5}{DID Compares Changes, Not Just Levels}

\slidecontent{The DID estimand subtracts the comparison group's before-after
change from the treated group's before-after change. This is why the method is
called ``difference-in-differences.''}

\[
\widehat{\delta}_{DID}
= (\bar{Y}_{T,post} - \bar{Y}_{T,pre})
- (\bar{Y}_{C,post} - \bar{Y}_{C,pre})
\]

\plannedfig{simple_did_2x2.png}{0.68}{Planned figure for Slide 5: the two-by-two DID comparison.}

\slideheading{6}{Regression Version of the Same Idea}

\slidecontent{The same two-by-two contrast can be estimated with a regression.
The coefficient on the interaction between treatment group and post period is
the DID estimate. Because the data are generated, we can compare the estimated
effect with the known simulated effect.}

\[
Y_{it}
= \alpha
+ \beta_1 Treat_i
+ \beta_2 Post_t
+ \delta(Treat_i \times Post_t)
+ \varepsilon_{it}.
\]

Two-by-two DID regression & 0.087 & 0.090 \\

\section*{Part B. TWFE and Event Studies}

\slideheading{7}{Two-Way Fixed Effects}

\slidecontent{Many applied DID papers estimate a two-way fixed effects (TWFE)
model. Unit fixed effects absorb stable differences across units, and year fixed
effects absorb shocks common to all units in a given year. In the simple
one-treatment-cohort case, TWFE maps closely onto the standard DID contrast.}

\[
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \delta D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}.
\]

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.62\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
$\alpha_i$ & Unit fixed effects: stable differences across units. \\
$\lambda_t$ & Year fixed effects: shocks common to all units in a year. \\
$D_{it}$ & Indicator that turns on after treatment starts. \\
$\delta$ & Average treatment effect estimate in the simple setup. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 7: pieces of the TWFE model.}
\end{table}

\clearpage

\slideheading{8}{Event Studies: Re-centering Time Around Treatment}

\slidecontent{Event studies extend DID by comparing outcomes at multiple time
points before and after treatment. The key move is to re-center time around
treatment entry. In the G\&T example, event time 0 is the year a student enters
G\&T; event time -1 is the year just before entry. In the simple synthetic data,
year 5 is treatment start, so year 4 is the reference year.}

\[
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t
+ \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k 1[t - G_i = k]
+ \varepsilon_{it}.
\]

\plannedfig{normalized_event_time_trends.png}{0.62}{Planned figure for Slide 8: outcomes centered at event time -1. Pre-treatment event times show whether treated and comparison groups were already diverging; post-treatment event times show how effects evolve after treatment.}

\slideheading{9}{What a Single TWFE Coefficient Can Hide}

\slidecontent{A single TWFE treatment coefficient can be too compressed if
effects change over time or if the design includes multiple treatment timings.
The event-study setup helps make those dynamics visible before moving into the
staggered DID case.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.53\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{What we estimate} & \textbf{What it can miss} \\
\midrule
One TWFE treatment coefficient & Whether effects grow, fade, or appear only after several years. \\
Average post-treatment effect & Differences across event time. \\
Simple treated/control comparison & Whether pre-treatment trends were already diverging. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption*{\footnotesize Planned table for Slide 9: why event-time thinking is useful before staggered DID.}
\end{table}
